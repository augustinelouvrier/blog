Uses of supercritical CO2 in cosmetics, a versatile solvent

Created on July 06, 2026

2026   ·   extraction   encapsulation   ·   long

What is a supercritical fluid?1

A supercritical fluid can be described as a fluid that does not have an observable difference between the gaseous and liquid states past a specific point called the critical point. The physical properties of a pure supercritical fluid compound show continuous rather than abrupt variations. Usually, supercritical fluids are used at densities that range from 10 to 80% of their liquid density and between 300-500 atm. They have liquid-like density while having gas-like diffusivity and a viscosity 10-100 times lower than liquids. Their liquid-like density means they can dissolve compounds and this is tunable via pressure and temperature changes.

CO2 is a common compound used in its supercritical form because it has a mild critical point, it is non-toxic and non-flammable, it is cheap and vents away easily without leaving residue.

Why it beats traditional extraction

Since scCO2 (supercritical CO2) is easily removable from extracts, the extracted product is of high quality and rarely needs refining1. Traditional extraction methods have long extraction times and large amounts of toxic solvents. They are also much less efficient and can degrade thermolabile compounds that are often present in natural materials. Plants are very widely used for the development of new ingredients and products in cosmeceutical and pharmaceutical applications. These plant extracts are valued in cosmeceuticals because of their antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-enzymatic and photoprotective properties2. This is why it is so important to keep them intact during extraction. scCO2 extraction is useful in this context because, for example, it can obtain a very broad lipophilic spectrum of plant constituents without any dilution or stress of high temperatures and does not leave any solvent residues. This is something that is virtually impossible with traditional methods. scCO2 is a nonpolar, hydrophobic solvent so low polarity molecules dissolve in it very easily.

Tunability and co-solvents

Since scCO2 is essentially nonpolar, neat CO2 pulls low-polarity compounds and misses the more polar phenolics or flavonoids. Adding ~5% ethanol to it raises its polarity and H-bonding enough to extend the range of compounds that is extracted3. A solute dissolves when its Hildebrand parameter is similar enough to the solvent’s value1. Adding ethanol to the CO2 raises the fluids effective Hildebrand parameter towards phenolic and flavonoid compounds. This is seen when comparing the mass of extracted material from dandelion flowers between neat CO2 and CO2 combined with 5% ethanol. It went from 4.8 g of extracted material out of 100 g of flowers to 7.2 g/100 g. It also reached equilibrium faster, 120 min for neat CO2 vs 90 min.

Also, hexane soxhlet was used as comparison to a traditional solvent which only managed to extract 6.6 g/100 g after 6 hours. The ethanol that is added to the CO2 increases the H-bonding of the supercritical phase which improves solubility and mass transfer of moderately polar phenolics or flavonoids that the neat CO2 does not extract as well.

The researchers measured the extract’s antioxidant content a few different ways. They measured how many phenolic and flavonoid compounds it contained, as well as its pigment content. They separately tested how well it mopped up free radicals. Adding just 5% ethanol as a co-solvent increased the phenolic content roughly threefold and the radical scavenging activity elevenfold. A small tweak to the solvent pulled out far more of the active compounds which is the point of the tunability of CO2.

The ethanol modified extract’s in vitro SPF (sun protection factor) rose with concentration to 49.5 at 1 mg/mL with UV-B absorption going up to 98%. The neat extract only reached ~13.9. This was only in vitro not in vivo, so has photoprotective potential not natural SPF 50.

Fragrance, extraction shapes the scent3

The same dandelion flower can give two distinct scents when extracted with neat CO2 compared to being extracted with the ethanol combined in the CO2.

In the dandelion plants, carvone remained the dominant compound through both extractions, it only decreased slightly (~16.5% to ~14%). Although carvone has two enantiomers that have different smells, the different extracts had the same composition of carvone. This is because the extraction is physical and cannot alter the fixed chirality. There is no racemisation route in this method so the smell differences in the two extracts are not due to these enantiomers smelling different to each other. Limonene was present at 3.8% in the flower but was then not detected in neat CO2. The 5% ethanol partially extracts it (0.3%) because it is less volatile than the scCO2. Limonene is extremely volatile, so it gets removed during depressurisation with the venting CO2. Heptadienals are absent in the flower but appear in the neat CO2 extract (~7%). These are likely secondary lipid-oxidation products so the aroma of the extract includes molecules that were not there before doing the extraction.

All of this shows that the same flower can give two different perfumes depending on whether ethanol is added to the scCO2. Neat CO2 will dissolve the low polarity species like terpene hydrocarbons or lightly oxygenated terpenoids. This extract is therefore a biased sample of the flower’s volatiles. Adding ethanol shifts this bias towards more polar, oxygenated, higher molecular weight compounds.

Even the neat extract does not smell like the flower because the selection means certain compounds are lost in the extraction. There are also some compounds formed during the extraction process which were not present in the original flower.

Beyond extraction: encapsulation4

Encapsulation is the process of adding an ingredient into a capsule or particle that has a coating material. This is done to improve the functionality or the performance of the core. This can tailor the capsule to protect or stabilise the release of a coated active ingredient.

The stabilisation of the active ingredients in a product can be done by isolating them in the core of a microcapsule to avoid any reaction or degradation. This encapsulation can enhance compatibility between ingredients. Active lipophilic compounds can be in an aqueous system without needing a co-solvent. It can also improve the sensorial properties of a product because it can mask the colour and odour of the active ingredients but still keeps their activity. Triggered release is made possible by encapsulation. Active ingredients are released in response to a stimulus.

Now scCO2 has been used as the processing medium for the formation of encapsulates. The subtle interaction between the scCO2 and the coating means that thin coating layers can be made without any defects. Then when scCO2 is removed, all the ingredients are solvent-free but well protected.

CO2 is useful in this context because it allows for greater control of the particle characteristics. It is environmentally friendly: there is less use of organic solvent and water and the process can be performed under mild conditions. However, there is quite a high cost for the commercial scale equipment and a lack of knowledge on this technique which makes it less accepted in industry applications.

Trade off and conclusions

The biggest drawback of supercritical CO2 is the cost. The upfront investment is quite high and most processes run in batches rather than continuously. Additionally, it still needs co-solvents when dealing with extractions of more polar compounds which scCO2 cannot do on its own very well. Also, this technique is still not very well known so companies are less likely to implement it.

For a cosmetics industry which is looking to reduce petrochemical solvents, scCO2 gives a real viable option as it can extract, fractionate and encapsulate. It is also very customisable by only changing the temperature or adding a co-solvent. The use of scCO2 in the extraction of natural fragrances gives a much lower energy expenditure compared to traditional methods like using hexane or distillation5. Being able to use a low temperature in extraction also means the organoleptic properties are as close as possible to the original properties from the raw materials.

Overall, this shows how new technologies can have a big impact on the sustainability of chemistry which is a problem many chemists try to tackle. Green Chemistry Principles 1 (waste prevention) and 5 (safer solvents and auxiliaries) are satisfied when using scCO2 extraction. This makes scCO2 stand out compared to its traditional alternatives which are usually toxic solvents that must be discarded as waste.

There is a caveat to be had because the two other relevant points from the Green Chemistry Principles are not fully satisfied using scCO2. Principle 12 (safer chemistry for accident prevention) is not entirely true because there are still risks associated with operating at 300-500 atm even though it is a safer solvent: no flammable or explosive solvent vapour. Principle 7 (use of renewable feedstocks) is only fully satisfied when the plant material is upcycled waste. When the biomass is a crop grown specifically to extract from then this principle is not truly followed. In fact, 25-50% of fruits and vegetables is lost between the production and consumers6. Fuit seeds, which are a common waste product from fruit production, contain many oils that are used in cosmetics. A source like this would be a good example for a renewable feedstock being used in scCO2 extraction in cosmetics.

Beyond extraction and encapsulation, recently scCO2 has been used in the extraction and analysis in the isolation of CBD and other cannabinoids from Cannabis sativa for skincare products7. scCO2 can be used in chromatography techniques to control the quality of finished cosmetic products.
